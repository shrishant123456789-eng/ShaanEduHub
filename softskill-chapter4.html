4.1 Basic

Fundamentals of Logic & Bias

Definition: Decision making is not just "choosing"; it is a structured process of identifying problems, gathering data, and weighing alternatives.

Key Concept: Cognitive Biases

  • Confirmation Bias: Seeking information that confirms pre-existing beliefs.
  • Anchoring Bias: Relying too heavily on the first piece of information (the "anchor").
  • Sunk Cost Fallacy: Continuing a venture because of past investment, despite poor future prospects.
Indian Context Example: Investing in a failing family business purely because "grandfather started it" is the Sunk Cost Fallacy.

Tool: Decision Trees

A tree-like graph of decisions and their possible consequences. Useful for sequential decision problems.

4.2 Medium

Structured Frameworks

1. SWOT Analysis

Strengths (Internal), Weaknesses (Internal), Opportunities (External), Threats (External).

2. Pareto Principle (80/20 Rule)

80% of consequences come from 20% of the causes. Focus on the vital few, not the trivial many.

3. The 5 Whys

Ask "Why?" five times to get to the root cause of a defect or problem.

Example: Why did the server crash? (High load) -> Why high load? (DDOS) -> Why DDOS successful? (No firewall) -> Why no firewall? (Budget cut) -> Root Cause: Lack of security budget prioritization.
4.3 Advanced

Strategic Thinking & AI

Systems Thinking

Viewing problems as parts of an overall system, rather than reacting to specific parts. Understanding feedback loops.

Risk Matrix

Plotting events by Probability (Likelihood) vs Impact (Consequence). Focus resources on High Probability + High Impact events.

AI-Enhanced Decision Making

Using data analytics and machine learning to predict outcomes. Warning: AI can hallucinate; human verification is essential.

4.4 Expert

Critical Judgment Under Uncertainty

Black Swan Theory

Events that are rare, have extreme impact, and are retrospectively predictable. (e.g., 2008 Financial Crisis, COVID-19).

First Principles Thinking

Boiling things down to the most fundamental truths and reasoning up from there, rather than reasoning by analogy ("doing what others do").

Elon Musk Example: Don't buy rockets (too expensive). Buy raw materials (aluminum, titanium) and build them. (SpaceX).

Assignment

Task: The Startup Pivot Decision

Scenario: You run a food delivery startup in Bangalore. Profits are low due to high fuel costs. You have two options:

  1. Switch to an electric bike fleet (High upfront cost, low running cost).
  2. Pivot to "Cloud Kitchens" only (No delivery fleet, partner with Swiggy/Zomato).

Requirement: Create a Cost-Benefit Analysis and a Risk Matrix for both options. Submit a 1-page report.

Real-Life Case Studies

Success: Netflix (Pivot)

Netflix used data to realize DVD rentals were declining. They cannibalized their own business to switch to streaming. Framework: Future-scenario planning.

Failure: Kodak (Ignorance)

Kodak invented the digital camera but suppressed it to protect film sales. They failed to use First Principles and ignored the changing technology curve.

Career & Expert Insight

  • ➤ Product Managers: Use decision matrices daily to prioritize features.
  • ➤ Data Scientists: Provide the statistical basis for decisions, but need domain knowledge to avoid bias.
  • ➤ Consultants (McKinsey/BCG): Master of the MECE (Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive) framework.
